How to play

Camelot and Cam are two-player games of knights and men. They share their moves and differ in the board, the number of pieces and how you win.

Play Camelot Play Cam

The moves

Players take turns, White first, and each turn moves one piece. Men and knights move in the same way, except that only knights can charge. Every move goes in a straight line in any of the eight directions: sideways, up and down, or diagonally.

The plain move

The canter

Leap over a friendly piece next to you onto the empty square just beyond it. You can chain several canters, turning between them, and stop whenever you like. A canter can't begin and end on the same square. (On this site a piece also can't canter over the same friend twice in one turn.)

The jump

Leap over an enemy piece next to you onto the empty square beyond, and capture it. Jumping is compulsory. If any of your pieces can jump, you must, though you choose which piece and which route. Once a piece starts jumping it must keep going for as long as it can jump again, and it may change direction at each jump.

The knight's charge

A knight (and only a knight) can canter one or more times and then jump one or more times, all in a single turn. A knight is never forced to charge, but a charge counts as a capture, so it can be how you meet the duty to jump. Men can chain canters or chain jumps, never a mixture.

Camelot

The classic game, on a board of 160 squares. Each side has ten men and four knights, with White at the bottom of the board.

Winning

  • Get two of your pieces into the enemy castle, the two squares at the far end of the board.
  • Or capture every enemy piece, as long as you have at least two pieces left.
  • Or leave your opponent with no legal move while you have at least two pieces.

The castles

  • Your own castle (the two squares behind your starting position): you can't step or canter into it, but you may jump into it. A piece that jumps in and can't keep jumping must leave on your next turn, ahead of any other move, and must jump out if it can.
  • The enemy castle: a piece that gets in ends its turn there and can never come out again. Each player may move a piece from one castle square to the other twice in the whole game.

Draws

  • You can agree a draw: offer one, and your opponent can accept or decline.
  • It is drawn at once when each side is down to a single piece or fewer.
  • Either player can claim a draw when the same position has come up three times with the same player to move.
  • Or when fifty moves each have passed with no capture and no piece reaching a castle.

Cam

A pocket version of Camelot, published by Parker Brothers in 1949. The moves, jumping and charging are the same. It is played on a much smaller board, and it is a race: one piece in the castle wins.

Playing here

  • On your turn, the pieces that can move are ringed. If you have to capture, only the pieces that can are ringed, and the hint above the buttons says so.
  • Click a piece, then click where it goes next: a gold dot is a plain move or a canter, and a red ring is a jump. Your route is drawn as a green line, with any captured pieces crossed out. Keep clicking for chains.
  • When the move is complete, press End turn. Undo takes back a click. The server only accepts legal moves.
  • Your opponent's last move is highlighted, and you can watch the route they are lining up before they confirm it.
  • The letters and numbers around the board name the squares, so you can talk about a move in the chat.
  • Games are saved. Close the page, lose your connection or restart your computer and you can come back from the menu. Your opponent shows as away meanwhile, and the game waits. There are no clocks.
  • Resign if you want to give up. By default a game nobody has touched for a week is cleared away, and a finished game is kept for a day.
  • Play again starts a new game against the same opponent with the colours swapped.

Where this site makes a call

The official text leaves a few things open. Here, a piece can't canter over the same friend twice in a turn (it stops endless loops), the two castle moves are counted per player, and if a player has no legal move while the other has fewer than two pieces, the game is drawn.
